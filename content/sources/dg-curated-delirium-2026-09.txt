# Sudden sleepiness or drowsiness can signal delirium

Excessive sleepiness that comes on suddenly — over hours or days — is not how
dementia normally changes. Dementia progresses slowly, over months. If a person
with dementia becomes much drowsier than usual, sleeps through most of the day,
is hard to wake, or seems unusually flat and withdrawn, a common cause is
delirium: a sudden disturbance of thinking and attention sitting on top of the
dementia. Delirium is not always agitated and restless. The quiet form —
sometimes called hypoactive delirium — shows up as sleepiness, drowsiness and
withdrawal, and it is the form most often missed, because a sleepy person is
easier to overlook than a distressed one. Delirium usually signals a treatable
physical problem, such as an infection (urinary tract infections are a common
trigger), dehydration, constipation, pain, or a recent change in medicines.
Left untreated, the underlying cause can become serious, so new excessive
sleepiness deserves prompt medical attention rather than watchful waiting. It
can look as though the dementia has jumped forward a stage overnight, but with
the cause found and treated, delirium is usually reversible.

# What to do about sudden drowsiness in a person with dementia

If a person with dementia becomes suddenly and unusually drowsy, treat it as a
change that needs same-day medical advice. Contact their GP and ask for an
appointment that day, or call Healthline on 0800 611 116 for free 24/7 advice
from a registered nurse. Mention how quickly the sleepiness came on, whether
they have signs of infection (fever, pain passing urine, a cough), how much
they are drinking, and whether any medicine was recently started, stopped or
changed — a sudden change after a medicine change deserves same-day advice in
its own right. Call 111 immediately if the person cannot be woken, is not
responding normally, has collapsed, or has new weakness, facial drooping or
trouble speaking. While waiting for advice, keep the person safe and
comfortable, offer fluids if they can swallow safely, and do not put the change
down to "the dementia getting worse" — a rapid change over hours or days almost
always has a physical cause that can be found and treated. Ordinary gradual
changes in sleep — napping a little more with age, or a slowly shifting sleep
pattern — are different, and can be discussed with the GP at a routine visit.
