**Question:** What is an Enduring Power of Attorney and does it cover both money and health decisions?

**Answer:**

An Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA) is a legal document that allows a person to appoint someone they trust to make decisions on their behalf if they become unable to do so themselves. In New Zealand, there are two types of EPAs, each covering different areas:

1. **EPA for Personal Care and Welfare**: This type of EPA is concerned with health and lifestyle decisions. It comes into effect only when a doctor determines that the person can no longer make safe and informed decisions for themselves. This EPA covers decisions about medical treatment and personal care.

2. **EPA for Property**: This EPA deals with financial matters, such as managing bank accounts, assets, and property. It can be set up to take effect immediately or only when the person loses capacity, depending on the individual's preference.

It's important to set up these documents while the person still has the mental capacity to understand and sign them. If this is not done in time, the family may need to apply to the Family Court to make decisions on their behalf, which can be a more complex and lengthy process [S3][S4]. For more guidance, you can contact Alzheimers NZ or your local Dementia NZ service.

## ISUP-F04 — psychosocial-model (fact)
**Manual reference:** Module 1 §3 — Kitwood's five psychological needs

**Question:** Someone told me about "five psychological needs" in dementia care from a person called Kitwood — what are they?

**Answer:**

Tom Kitwood identified five psychological needs that are important in dementia care, which overlap and collectively fulfil a central need for love. These needs are:

1. **Comfort**: This involves ensuring the person is free from distress and pain, both physically and emotionally. It includes making sure they are not hungry, too cold, or too warm.

2. **Attachment**: Having a connection to familiar people or places provides a sense of safety and security. This need is about maintaining relationships and connections that are meaningful to the person.

3. **Inclusion**: People with dementia need to feel included in their community and social circles. This can be challenging due to communication difficulties, but it's important to support their involvement in conversations and activities.

4. **Occupation**: Being engaged in activities that are personally meaningful provides a sense of purpose and helps maintain self-esteem. Without this, individuals may experience boredom and apathy.

5. **Identity**: Understanding and honouring a person's life history and roles in society helps maintain their sense of self. Recognising their unique identity supports their other psychological needs, such as attachment and inclusion.

These needs highlight the importance of person-centred care, focusing on the individual rather than just the disease [S1].
